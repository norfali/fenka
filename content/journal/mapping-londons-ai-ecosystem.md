I wanted to see where London’s AI teams actually work. Putting those workplaces on a map sounded like a straightforward way to answer the question. It also gave me an excuse to build the sort of map I’d want to spend time with, where you can follow the Thames across the city and drop into a district to see how it fits together.

## Pick the right detail for each view

At city scale, the Thames is enough to tell you which way you’re looking. Parks, transport and the outline of Greater London do most of the remaining work. At district scale you want different cues. In Shoreditch, the Tea Building and the railway viaduct say more about where you are than another road label would.

The Atlas uses OpenStreetMap to anchor both views. In the districts, building footprints become isometric forms. Heights are mapped where available and estimated where needed. There’s room for interpretation in how tall something looks or what gets labelled, but the streets and buildings give the scene a geography you can check. The small transport details grew out of that. A train has a line to follow and a reason to disappear when the track goes under cover.

## What does a pin claim?

Company locations were the part I could least afford to treat casually. An incorporation address can be accurate for a company and useless to someone trying to find its team. A press release might announce a move before it happens. A London address on a contact page might tell you less than a building name in a recent office announcement.

I started thinking of each marker as a claim with evidence attached. The Atlas records the source for a workplace address and ties the pin to a building footprint. When an address can’t be confirmed, I can keep researching without putting a plausible-looking dot on the map. A good visual treatment makes a pin look authoritative. That gives me more reason to be careful about what earns one.

## Leave room for the city to change

Companies move and the evidence goes stale. A map that’s easy to correct and extend is more useful to me than one that tries to appear finished. I want to be able to revisit a district, follow an entry back to its source and understand why it was placed there.

I still enjoy the miniature trains. They’re a reminder that this began as something I wanted to explore. The directory gives me a reason to open the Atlas, and the city gives me a reason to look around once I’m there.
